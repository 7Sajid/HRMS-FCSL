Section 1

What this software is for

Today, most of FCSL's staff information lives in three places: paper files in a cabinet, spreadsheets on somebody's computer, and people's memory. That works until it doesn't.

The five problems it is meant to fix

1 — Nobody knows when a licence is about to expire

An Authorised Representative's certificate has an expiry date. If it lapses, that person legally cannot deal with clients. Right now this is tracked by whoever remembers. The system will warn four months before every expiry, automatically, to the right people.

2 — Employee documents are scattered and incomplete

NID copies, certificates, appointment letters, bank details. When BSEC or an auditor asks for a staff file, someone has to hunt. The system keeps one complete folder per person, and shows at a glance who is missing what.

3 — Leave runs on paper and verbal approval

An employee asks a manager, the manager says yes, and HR may or may not hear about it. The system makes every request written, timed, and traceable — and the employee can see the decision without walking to anyone's desk.

4 — Branch attendance arrives late and in different shapes

Each branch sends its monthly attendance in its own format. The system gives every branch manager the same sheet, the same deadline, and one button to submit it.

5 — There is no record of who approved what

For a BSEC-regulated brokerage this is the serious one. Every approval, rejection and document view is recorded with the person's name and the exact time, and cannot be edited afterwards.

What it is not

This is not an accounting system and it does not pay salaries. It holds people, their papers, their time off, and the company's decisions about them. Nothing more. Section 11 lists exactly what has been left out.

Why RM and AR are grouped with Employee

An RM or AR uses exactly the same screens as any other employee. This matters when we come to the question of who approves an HR Executive's own leave — see Section 10.

Section 3

The locked door

If you remember one thing from this document, remember this. It is the idea the entire system is built on, and it applies to every single person — the newest junior clerk and the HR Head alike.

The rule in one sentence

When your account is first created, you can do exactly one thing: upload your documents. Everything else stays locked until the person above you checks those documents and approves them.

The draft describes this five separate times — once for the employee, once for the RM/AR, once for the manager, once for the HR Executive and once for the HR Head — which is why it reads like five different systems. It is one system. Only the contents of Stage 2 differ.

Problem — who creates the first account?

The draft says the Super Admin approves the HR Head, but it never says who creates or approves the Super Admin. On the day the system goes live there is nobody inside it to do anything.

Recommended answer: the very first Super Admin account is created once, by hand, during installation, by the person setting up the system. It skips the locked door because there is nobody above it. From that moment the system creates the HR Head, the HR Head creates the HR Executives, and the chain runs normally. That first account should belong to a named director, and the fact that it was created outside the normal process should itself be written into the permanent record.

Why the locked door is worth the inconvenience

It would be easier to let everyone in immediately and chase the paperwork later. That is how most offices end up with half-empty staff files. Putting the wall at the start means:

  • Nobody is ever in the system without a complete file — the file is the entry ticket.
  • HR checks documents once, at the moment the person is most motivated to hand them over.
  • When an auditor asks for staff records, there is no such thing as an incomplete one.
The cost of this design, stated honestly

A new joiner cannot use the system on day one. They log in, see an upload screen, and wait for HR. If HR is slow, the person sits blocked. The system therefore shows HR a queue of people waiting, sorted by how long they have been waiting, and sends a reminder if anyone has been waiting more than two working days.

Section 4

A new employee's first week

This is the whole onboarding process told as a story. Everything in it comes from the draft; only the order and the plain wording are new.

1Before day one — HR creates the account

The interview is finished and the person has accepted. An HR Executive opens the system and creates an account using three pieces of information only: name, email address, and mobile number. Nothing else is needed yet. HR also chooses what kind of user this will be — employee, RM, AR, manager — because that decides what will eventually unlock.

2The password is handed over by a person

The draft is specific: "This login credentials will be sent to the person manually." So the system generates a temporary password and HR passes it on by hand, by phone, or in the offer meeting — not by automatic email. The password works once. The first thing the system asks for after that first login is a new password of the person's own choosing.

Why this is a reasonable choice

Sending a first password by email means anyone who can read that inbox can become that employee. Handing it over in person removes that risk entirely. The trade-off is that HR has one more small task per joiner. For a company of FCSL's size that is a fair trade.

3First login — the upload screen

The new joiner logs in and sees the locked panel from Section 3. One screen, one job: upload documents. Each required document is a labelled box. As each file goes in, the box turns green. A progress line at the top reads, for example, "6 of 9 required documents uploaded". The Submit button stays grey until every required box is green.

The full list of what must be uploaded is on the next page.

Two situations the draft does not cover

The joiner never uploads anything. The account simply sits at Stage 1. It cannot be used for anything, so it is harmless — but it should not sit there for ever. After thirty days with nothing uploaded, HR is told, and can either chase the person or deactivate the account.

The temporary password is never used. It expires after seven days. HR generates a new one and hands it over again. An unused temporary password that stays valid for months is a way into the system that nobody is watching.

What HR does not have to do at this stage

HR does not type in the employee's personal details, does not create the employee ID, and does not choose the branch — none of that happens until the documents arrive and are checked. Creating an account is genuinely a three-field job. Everything else is decided at the moment of approval, when the evidence is in front of the person deciding.

Section 4, continued

Reading, checking, and opening the panel

4The system reads the documents and fills the form

This is the part of the draft that says information "will be collected automatically from their uploaded documents". When the NID and certificates go in, the system reads them and fills in full name, father's name, mother's name, date of birth, gender and nationality by itself.

How it will work — reading, but never trusting blindly

Every field the system fills in this way is marked with a small amber dot meaning "read from a document, not yet confirmed". HR sees the original scan on one side of the screen and the filled field on the other, and confirms or corrects each one. The amber dot only turns green when a human has agreed with it. Nothing enters an employee's permanent record on the word of a machine alone.

Religion, marital status and anything the documents do not contain stay blank for HR to fill in.

5HR reviews — the most important half-hour

The joiner presses Submit and the file lands in HR's queue. An HR Executive opens it and works down a checklist: is each document readable, is it the right document, do the auto-read fields match the scan, are the bank details complete. Then one of two things happens.

Approve

HR assigns the employee ID, sets the branch, sets who the person reports to, and presses Approve. The full panel opens the moment the person next refreshes their screen. They get an in-app notification and an email.

Send back

HR marks the specific documents that are wrong and writes a reason — "NID back side is blurred, please re-scan". Only those boxes reopen. Everything already accepted stays accepted, so nothing is uploaded twice.

6The panel opens

The employee logs in and the four pages described in Section 5 are there. Their file is complete, their ID exists, they are attached to a branch and a manager, and they can apply for leave from that day forward. The whole sequence, if HR is prompt, takes about two days.

What the permanent record now contains

Account created by [name] at [time]. Documents submitted at [time]. Two fields corrected by [name]. Approved by [name] at [time]. Employee ID issued. None of these lines can ever be edited or deleted by anybody, including the Super Admin.

Section 5 · Panel 1 of 5

What an Employee sees

Employee, Relationship Manager and Authorised Representative all use this panel.

Page 1 — My personal information

A read-only summary of who the person is: employee ID, full name, father's and mother's name, date of birth, photograph, gender, nationality, religion and marital status. The employee looks at it; HR edits it. If something is wrong, there is a Request a correction button that sends a short note to HR rather than changing anything directly.

Page 2 — My documents

Every file the person has uploaded, listed with the date it went in and whether HR accepted it. The draft is precise about the permission here: view only, but they can still upload anything they did not upload before. So an employee can add a missing training certificate at any time, but cannot replace or delete a document HR has already accepted. A new upload goes to HR as a small approval of its own.

Page 3 — Emergency contacts

Both contacts, displayed. The draft marks this page view only.

Problem — nobody can ever update this

Emergency phone numbers change constantly. Marked view-only in every panel, this page slowly fills with numbers that no longer work — and it is the one page that matters on the worst day of somebody's career. Recommended: let the employee edit it, and route the change to HR as a one-click approval. Full history kept, so the old number is never simply lost.

Page 4 — Leave and monthly attendance

Three things on one page:

  • Apply for leave — pick the type, the dates, write a reason, attach a medical certificate if needed. The system shows how many days remain of each type before the person submits.
  • My leave this month — every application with its status: waiting, approved, refused, withdrawn — and who decided it.
  • My attendance — the month laid out as a calendar. This appears only after HR submits the month, exactly as the draft specifies. Before that, the employee sees "Not yet published".

Always available — calendar and notes

The draft ends with "Calendar and Note taking Option for every one". Every panel carries a personal calendar showing company holidays, the person's own approved leave, and any private notes they write. Notes are private to the person who writes them and are not visible to HR.

Section 5 · Panel 2 of 5

What an RM or AR sees that is different

The four pages are identical to the employee panel. Two things are added.

A stricter document list at the start

Where an ordinary employee may leave experience and release letters out, an RM or AR must supply them. An AR must additionally upload the AR certificate itself, and enter two dates with it: the issue date and the expiry date. Those two dates are the reason this role is treated separately at all.

A licence panel that counts down

The AR's own page shows the certificate, its number, when it was issued, when it expires, and how long is left — in plain words, not just a date: "Valid. Expires 14 March 2027 — 6 months and 11 days remaining."

The four-month warning, from the AR's side

Four months before expiry the wording changes to "Renewal due" and turns amber, a notification appears in the bell, and an email goes out. The same warning goes to the person's manager, to HR, and onto the HR Head's dashboard on the same day — so it does not depend on one person noticing.

If the date passes without renewal, the status turns red and reads "Expired". The system does not quietly hide it; an expired licence stays visible at the top of the HR register until it is resolved.

Why four months, and not one

Because renewal is not instant. The paperwork has to be prepared, signed, submitted, and processed by the regulator. Four months gives room for all of that plus one round of something going wrong. A one-month warning would arrive too late to be useful.

A question for FCSL

Should the system do anything beyond warning when a licence expires — for example, mark the person as unable to be assigned client work? The draft does not say. Our recommendation is to warn loudly but not to block, because blocking a person's account over a date-entry mistake causes more damage than it prevents. This is listed as an open question in Section 12.

Section 5 · Panel 3 of 5

What a Manager sees

Manager, Department Head and Divisional Manager share this panel.

A manager gets all four employee pages — because a manager is also an employee who takes leave and has a file — plus one more page that is entirely about other people.

Page 5 — Approvals and requests

Three sections, all on one screen.

Leave waiting for me

Every leave application from the manager's own team. Each row shows who, what type, which dates, how many days, the reason, and how much leave that person has left. Two buttons: Grant or Deny. A denial requires a written reason — the system will not accept an empty one. The employee sees the outcome immediately.

Raise a requisition

One form with a type dropdown: office supplies, IT equipment, money or expense, or a request for new staff. The form changes depending on the type chosen — a stationery request asks for quantity, an expense request asks for an amount and a bill. It then travels to HR and, above a value FCSL sets, to the Super Admin.

Submit the branch attendance sheet

Once a month. The whole branch appears as a grid — people down the side, days across the top. The manager marks present, absent, late, or on leave. Days already approved as leave are filled in automatically and cannot be contradicted. When the manager presses Submit, the sheet locks and goes to HR. After that only HR can change it, and every change is recorded.

What a manager cannot see

A manager sees their own team and nothing else. Not another branch, not another department, not salary, not another manager's team. If somebody moves between teams, the old manager keeps access to the historical records of the period when that person reported to them, and loses access to everything after the transfer date.

Unfinished in the draft

The draft says the manager grants or denies leave. It never says whether HR then has to agree as well. Both designs are defensible — manager's word final is faster; HR countersigning is safer for leave-balance accuracy. This is question 3 in Section 12 and needs an answer before the leave module is built.

What an HR Executive sees

The four employee pages, plus three working pages. This is the person who does the daily HR work.

Page 5 — Set up an employee

The screen used at the moment of approving a new joiner, and any time somebody's situation changes. Four jobs on it:

  • Issue the employee ID. The draft says "HR will assign / automate". The system will propose the next ID automatically in a fixed format; HR can override it if there is a reason. The format itself is question 1 in Section 12.
  • Put the person in a category — the grade or staff class they belong to.
  • Set who they report to — pick their manager.
  • Set their branch. The draft calls out the important case explicitly: if a branch is changed for any manager or employee. Transfers happen, and the system must handle them as a dated event, not by overwriting. The old branch keeps the history; the new branch takes over from the transfer date.

Page 6 — Find anybody

One searchable list of every employee, RM, AR and manager, with filters down the side: branch, department, role, category, manager, joining date, status. Type a name or an ID into the box and the list narrows as you type. Click a row to open that person's full file. The result can be exported to a spreadsheet — and every export is recorded, because a list of every employee's details leaving the building is exactly the kind of event an auditor asks about.

Page 7 — The AR licence register

Every AR in the company on one screen, with issue date, expiry date, and a status that colours itself: green for valid, amber once inside four months, red once expired. Sorted so the most urgent sits at the top. HR uploads renewed certificates here and enters the new dates, and the countdown restarts. This single page is the answer to problem 1 in Section 1.

Section 5 · Panel 5 of 5

What the HR Head sees

Everything the HR Executive has, plus five more pages. This is the widest panel in the system. Note one detail from the draft: the HR Head's own leave application goes upward, to the Super Admin — the HR Head cannot approve their own leave.

Pages 1 to 6 — the same as HR Executive

Personal information, documents, emergency contacts, own leave and attendance, employee setup, and the search page. Identical screens, wider reach: the HR Head sees every branch.

Page 7 — Reports and analysis

The draft asks for "Employee + Attendance + etc analysis report". In practice that means a dashboard answering the questions a Head of HR is actually asked:

  • Headcount — total, by branch, by department, by grade; who joined and who left this month.
  • Attendance — the branches with the most absence, the most lateness, the lowest attendance.
  • Leave — days taken by type; who is close to exhausting their entitlement; who has taken none at all, which is itself worth knowing in a brokerage.
  • Documents — which staff files are still incomplete, and how long they have been that way.
  • Licences — how many AR certificates expire in the next three, six and twelve months.

Every report can be filtered by date range and branch, and exported.

Page 8 — Approvals

One inbox holding everything waiting for the HR Head: leave applications that have reached this level, and requisitions from every department. Each item shows who raised it, when, what the manager below already decided, and the full history of the request. Approve or refuse, with a written reason required for a refusal.

Why one inbox rather than several

The single most common failure of an approval system is that requests sit unseen in a screen nobody opens. One inbox, one counter on the menu, one email digest each morning. If a request has been waiting more than three working days it moves to the top and turns amber.

Section 5, continued

The HR Head's last three pages

Page 9 — Compliance and show-cause letters

The disciplinary process, carried out inside the software instead of on paper. The draft describes it in one line; here is what it means in practice:

  1. The HR Head opens a show-cause against a named employee and writes the letter, choosing from a set of approved templates so the wording and legal footing stay consistent.
  2. The employee receives it — a notification, an email, and a red banner across their panel that does not go away.
  3. The employee writes a reply inside the system and may attach evidence. The draft asks for the reply as a PDF; the system produces that PDF automatically from what they write, so nothing has to be typed in Word and scanned.
  4. The HR Head reads the reply and records the outcome: closed with no action, warning issued, escalated. The letter, the reply and the outcome are stored together in that person's file permanently.
This page needs stricter privacy than any other

A show-cause is visible to the HR Head, the employee concerned, and nobody else by default — not even their manager, unless the HR Head chooses to include them. The system should record every single time this record is opened, and by whom.

Page 10 — Branches

Create a branch, record its details — name, code, address, phone, opening date, branch manager — and see who is attached to it: managers, employees, RMs and ARs. Closing a branch is not deletion; it is marked closed on a date, and its people must be moved somewhere else first. The history survives.

Page 11 — Trading terminals (TWS)

A register of every Trader Work Station ID the company holds, and who is currently using it. For each terminal: the terminal ID, which exchange, the branch it sits in, the person it is assigned to, the date it was assigned, and its status — active, suspended or surrendered.

Why this belongs in the HR system and not with IT

A trading terminal is tied to a person, and that person's right to use it depends on their AR licence being valid and their still being employed. Keeping the terminal register next to the licence register and the staff list means the system can answer the awkward question by itself: is anybody holding an active terminal whose licence has expired, or who has already left the company? That question is very hard to answer when the two lists live in different departments.

And the Super Admin

The draft never describes this panel, so here is what it must contain to make the rest work: approval of the HR Head's account and of the HR Head's own leave; final approval of requisitions above whatever value FCSL sets; the ability to create and deactivate accounts of any kind; and read-only access to the permanent record of every action taken in the system. It is a small panel used rarely, and that is correct — a Super Admin who is in the system every day is a Super Admin whose password will eventually be shared.

The rule about deleting things

Nothing is ever deleted

A wrong document is superseded, not removed — the new version becomes current and the old one stays underneath, marked replaced, with the reason and the name of whoever replaced it. A person who leaves is marked as left on a date; their record stays whole. This is not caution for its own sake. A brokerage that cannot produce a former employee's file when asked has a real problem, and the cheapest way to guarantee it can is to make deletion impossible in the first place.

How documents are kept safe

  • Files are stored in a private area with no public web address. There is no link that works if someone forwards it.
  • Opening a document produces a temporary link that stops working after a few minutes.
  • Every view of a document is recorded — who looked at whose NID, and when.
  • Files are encrypted where they are stored, so a copy of the raw storage is useless on its own.
A decision FCSL must make

How long should a former employee's documents be kept after they leave? There is a legal minimum and there is a point beyond which holding somebody's NID copy is a liability rather than an asset. This needs an answer from your compliance officer — it is question 12 in Section 12 — and once decided, the system enforces it automatically.

Section 6 · Part 2

Leave

The part of the system used most often, by the most people, and therefore the part that has to be simplest.

Applying

The employee picks a leave type, picks the dates on a calendar, and writes a reason. Before they press send, the system already tells them three things: how many working days this actually costs (weekends and public holidays are excluded automatically), how many days of that type they have left, and who the request is going to. No surprises after the fact.

What the system checks before accepting an application
  • Are the dates in the future, or is this a late application that needs a reason?
  • Does the person have enough days of that type left?
  • Do these dates overlap an application they have already made?
  • Does the type require an attachment — a medical certificate for extended sick leave?
  • Are too many people in the same team already off on those dates?

Deciding

The application appears in the manager's page 5 the moment it is sent. Grant or Deny, with a written reason required for a denial. The employee is notified immediately and the days are taken off their balance the moment it is granted — not at the end of the month.

Withdrawing

Plans change. An employee can withdraw an application that has not yet been decided, and can request cancellation of one already granted if the dates have not started. The days go back onto the balance and the record shows what happened.

Two things the draft does not define — both blocking

The leave types and how many days each carries. Casual, sick, earned, maternity, and whatever else FCSL grants. The Bangladesh Labour Act 2006 sets minimums; a company may be more generous. Without this list, the system cannot count anything.

The weekly off days and the public holiday calendar. Until the system knows which days are non-working, it cannot tell whether a Thursday-to-Sunday leave is two days or four. Nothing in the leave or attendance modules can be built correctly without it.

How balances are handled underneath

Entitlements are kept as dated rules rather than fixed numbers written into the software. When the law changes, or FCSL changes its policy, the new rule is added with the date it takes effect. Last year's leave is still calculated on last year's rule; this year's on this one. Nobody has to rewrite the program, and old records do not silently change meaning.

Why the sheet locks on submission

An attendance record that can be changed quietly afterwards is worth nothing in a dispute. Once submitted, only HR can change it, only with a reason, and the change is visible forever alongside the original entry. The manager can see what HR changed and why.

The honest limitation of this approach

Attendance is only as accurate as the manager filling it in. There is no independent evidence — no punch, no photo, no location. This is a genuine weakness and it should be understood as a deliberate choice rather than an oversight: it costs nothing, needs no hardware in any branch, and can be replaced later. If FCSL later installs punch devices, the system is built so that machine data can feed the same grid, with the manager correcting missing punches instead of typing everything. Compliance — the show-cause process

Described in full in Section 5. In summary: the HR Head writes a show-cause letter from a template, the employee is notified and sees it as a banner they cannot dismiss, the employee writes a reply which the system turns into a PDF automatically, and the HR Head records the outcome. Letter, reply and outcome stay together in the person's file permanently.

What this replaces

Today this is a printed letter, a signature on a receipt copy, a typed reply, and a photocopy in a file — with no reliable way to prove afterwards when the letter was actually received. The system timestamps delivery and receipt, which protects the employee and the company equally.

Section 6 · Underneath everything

The permanent record

This part has no screen of its own and most people will never think about it. It is the reason the system can be trusted.

Every action that changes something, or reveals somebody's private information, writes one line that can never be edited or deleted — not by HR, not by the Super Admin, not by the person who built the software.

What a line looks like, in plain words

"Rahim Uddin (HR Executive) approved the documents of Karim Hossain at 11:42 on 14 March 2026."
"Nasreen Akter (HR Head) opened the NID of Salma Begum at 09:15 on 2 April 2026."
"Branch attendance for Motijheel, March 2026, corrected by Rahim Uddin — 2 entries changed — reason: manager reported wrong dates."

What gets recorded

  • Every approval and every refusal, with the reason given.
  • Every document uploaded, replaced or viewed.
  • Every change to somebody's personal, bank or employment details — the old value and the new one.
  • Every login, and every failed login attempt.
  • Every export of employee data to a spreadsheet.

Only the Super Admin and the HR Head can read this record, and they can only read it. It is the first thing an auditor will ask for, and having it means the answer to "who approved this?" is never a matter of memory.

Calendar and notes

The draft's closing line asks for "Calendar and Note taking Option for every one". Every panel, from the newest employee to the Super Admin, carries:

  • A calendar showing public holidays, weekly offs, the person's own approved leave, and — for managers and HR — who in their team is away on any given day. That last view alone prevents most of the accidental double-booking that causes leave to be refused.
  • A notes area, private to the person who writes it. HR cannot read an employee's notes and an employee cannot read HR's. This must be stated plainly to everyone, because a private notepad that turns out not to be private is worse than no notepad at all.

How an approval travels

Four kinds of thing need somebody's agreement: a new person's documents, a leave application, a requisition, and a branch attendance sheet. Each follows a fixed path.

What happens when nobody responds

Every request that has waited more than three working days moves to the top of the approver's inbox and turns amber, and a reminder email goes out. After a week, the person's own superior is told as well. The system never silently approves anything on its own — a request left alone stays open, and becomes progressively harder to ignore.

Section 10 · Needs your decision

Nine problems found in the draft

These came out of reading the requirement document line by line. None of them is a criticism of the thinking behind it — the draft is a good description of what FCSL wants. They are places where, if built exactly as written, the system would not work. Each has a recommended fix; the decision is yours.

Problem 1 — the new joiner is asked for documents only FCSL can produce

Before a new joiner can get past the locked door, the draft requires them to upload their appointment letter, joining letter and confirmation letter. FCSL issues all three. On day one the person has none of them — a confirmation letter usually arrives six months later. As written, nobody can ever complete onboarding.

Recommended fix: split the list in two. The joiner uploads only what they personally hold — CV, NID, photograph, certificates, previous experience or release letter, bank details, and the AR certificate if they have one. HR uploads the company-issued letters into the same file afterwards, as they are produced. The person's file still ends up complete; it just fills in the order real life fills it.

Problem 2 — clearance documents are on the joining checklist

Clearance paperwork is produced when somebody leaves. It cannot exist when they join. Recommended fix: remove it from the joining list entirely and move it to the exit process — which brings us to problem 7.

Problem 3 — the emergency contact can never be updated

Page 3 is marked view only in all five panels. Nobody — not the employee, not their manager — can change it after the first day. Emergency numbers change when people move house, change SIM, or when the named person dies. Over three years this page becomes a list of numbers that no longer connect, and it is the one page that matters on the worst day of somebody's career.

Recommended fix: let the employee edit it. The change goes to HR as a one-click approval, and the previous version is kept. Small change, and it is the difference between a page that works and a page that only looks like it works.

Problem 4 — nobody can create the first Super Admin

The draft says the Super Admin approves the HR Head, but never says who creates or approves the Super Admin. On installation day the system is empty and there is nobody inside it to do anything. Recommended fix: the first Super Admin is created once by hand during installation, skipping the locked door because there is nothing above it, and that fact is itself written permanently into the record. From there the normal chain runs.

Problem 5 — an RM or AR cannot request anything

The requisition page is given to managers, HR and above. Employees, RMs and ARs have no way to ask for a laptop, a chair, or reimbursement of a client-visit taxi fare. This is almost certainly an oversight rather than a policy. Recommended fix: give everyone the ability to raise a requisition; it goes to their manager first, so no manager loses control of anything.

Section 10, continued

Problems 6 to 9

Problem 6 — the leave approval chain stops halfway

The draft says a manager grants or denies a team member's leave. It then stops. Two things are left unanswered, and both block the leave module:

  • Once a manager grants leave, is that final, or must HR agree as well?
  • Who approves an HR Executive's own leave? They have no manager in the draft, and their own panel has no approval page.

Recommended fix: the manager's decision is final and HR is informed rather than asked — it is faster, and HR still holds the balances and can query anything odd. The HR Executive's leave goes to the HR Head. But this is a policy question, not a technical one, and FCSL should choose.

Problem 7 — there is no way to leave the company

Resignation, release letters and clearance documents appear all through the draft, but no panel has a page for handling somebody's departure. Yet an employee leaving is one of the highest-risk moments in a brokerage: their trading terminal must be surrendered, their AR licence status changed, their system access closed the same day, and clearance obtained from every department.

Recommended fix: add a short exit process — resignation recorded, last working day set, a clearance checklist across departments, terminal and licence released, access closed automatically on the last day, release letter issued, record marked as left but kept whole. It is a small amount of work and it closes the biggest hole in the design.

Problem 8 — the employee ID format is undefined

The draft says only "HR will assign / automate". Before anything can be automated, somebody must decide the shape. Is there an existing series to continue? Should the ID carry the branch or joining year, or be a plain running number? Are IDs ever reused after somebody leaves — they should not be. A five-minute decision that blocks the first thing built.

Problem 9 — the draft contradicts itself in three places
  • The HR Head section opens with "HR exe have to submit before getting the Employee panel" — copied from the HR Executive section above it.
  • The same section lists "ID — Super Admin" under the HR Head's own personal information, where it should read HR Head.
  • The alternate emergency contact asks for four fields on the form but displays five — date of birth appears only on the view page.
Why these are worth the pages they take up

Problems 1, 2 and 4 each stop the system working on its first day. Problem 7 would be discovered six months in, when the first person resigns and their trading terminal stays live. Finding them now costs a conversation; finding them later costs a rewrite.

Section 13

How it would be built, and in what order

Four phases. Each one ends with something FCSL can actually use, rather than everything arriving at once at the end. If the project stopped after any phase, what had been delivered would still be worth having.

Phase 1 — Accounts, documents and the locked door

Logging in, the five panels, the upload screen, reading NID and certificates with HR confirming, HR's review queue, employee IDs, branches, and the permanent record underneath it all.

At the end of this phase FCSL can onboard every existing member of staff and finally have one complete, searchable set of employee files. That alone solves problems 2 and 5 from Section 1.

Phase 2 — Leave and attendance

Leave types and balances, applying, approving, the holiday calendar, the branch attendance grid, HR verification and monthly publication.

At the end of this phase paper leave forms stop. This is the phase every employee will notice. It cannot start until questions 5 and 6 are answered.

Phase 3 — Requisition and compliance

The requisition form and its approval route, show-cause letters, employee replies as automatic PDFs, and outcomes recorded in the file.

At the end of this phase the internal paper trail is fully inside the system.

Phase 4 — The registers and the reports

The AR licence register with its four-month warning, the trading terminal register, and the HR Head's dashboard and exports.

At the end of this phase nobody ever again discovers a licence expiry by accident.

What it will be built with, in one paragraph

A single website, working equally on a desktop in the office and on a phone in a branch. One database holding everything, with the privacy rules from Section 9 enforced inside the database itself rather than only on the screen. Documents in private encrypted storage with links that expire. Reading NIDs and certificates handled by an artificial-intelligence service, with every field it produces marked unconfirmed until a human agrees. Email for the alerts. Nightly automatic backups. Nothing exotic — these are ordinary, well-proven choices, deliberately, because an HR system needs to still be running in five years.

The first three things to do

1. Read Section 10 and decide on the nine problems — particularly problems 1, 4 and 7. 2. Answer questions 5 and 6 in Section 12, the leave types and the holiday calendar, because they block the largest phase. 3. Name the Super Admin. Everything else can be settled while phase 1 is being built.

In closing

What this document is asking of you

The requirement draft was a good piece of thinking. It knew what FCSL needed: complete staff files, leave that leaves a trace, attendance that arrives on time, and a way of never again being surprised by a licence that has quietly expired. What it did not have was a shape — a way for somebody outside the room where it was written to read it and understand how the system behaves.

That shape turns out to be simple. One website. Five kinds of user. One locked door that everybody passes through once. Nine working parts. One permanent record underneath that nobody can edit.

Reading it closely also turned up nine places where the design as written would not survive contact with a real Monday morning — a new joiner who cannot upload a letter that does not exist yet, an emergency number that can never be corrected, a first account that nobody can create, and an employee who resigns while their trading terminal stays live. Those are in Section 10, each with a recommendation.

Mark up this document wherever it describes FCSL incorrectly — that is more useful than agreement. Then answer as many of the twelve questions in Section 12 as you can.
